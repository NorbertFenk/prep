CDNs: how google.com is a thousand places at once

Lesson 0011 · How the Internet Works · ~15 min · builds on lessons 0002, 0007, 0008

Your 60-second answer ends at a server. Sharp interviewers ask: "which one? Google has thousands." The answer is a CDN — and it interleaves three chapters you already own: DNS (lesson 2), caching (lesson 7), load balancing (lesson 8).

Why distance costs milliseconds

Packets move through fiber at roughly 200 km per millisecond — fast, but finite. Every beat of your journey pays for it: TCP's handshake, TLS's round trips, the request itself. Sit 10,000 km from the server and even a flawless site stacks dozens of cross-ocean round trips per page. The fix isn't faster packets — it's shorter wires. Put copies of the content near the user.

Anatomy: one origin, many edges

  • Origin server: where the content actually lives. Users normally never touch it.
  • Edge servers (deployed in points of presence, often at internet exchange points): cache copies of assets worldwide (Cloudflare).
  • Cache miss? That edge pulls from the origin once, keeps the copy, serves every neighbor after. Freshness obeys the same Cache-Control rules from lesson 7 — stale entries get revalidated before reuse.
Find DNS: shop.com → CNAME → CDN's DNS → nearby edge IP (not the origin)
Connect TCP+TLS to that edge — short RTT makes both handshakes cheap
Ask GET /logo.png → edge HIT: served locally · MISS: pull origin, store, serve

Beyond speed: edges offload origin bandwidth, absorb traffic spikes, and soak up DDoS attacks before they reach home (Cloudflare). Sound familiar? An edge is a reverse proxy plus a cache — lesson 8's front door, replicated worldwide — and inside each data center, load balancers and health checks run exactly as you learned there.

Getting routed to the right edge

1
Anycast: one IP address announced from many data centers at once. Internet routing itself delivers each packet to the topologically nearest site; if a center drowns or dies, traffic reroutes to the next closest automatically (Cloudflare).
2
DNS-based routing: the site's authoritative DNS hands out a pointer into the CDN's zone; the CDN's nameservers then answer with an IP chosen by where the resolver is asking from. Lesson 2's chain picks the door; anycast or geography decides which one opens.

Upgrade your 60-second answer

One clause transforms it from textbook to senior: after the DNS beat, add "— which resolves me to a nearby CDN edge, not some distant origin, so every later round trip stays cheap." Bonus point: mention TLS terminates at the edge, echoing your reverse-proxy knowledge.

Model answers (peek only after trying)

Where it sits: "Right after DNS. The lookup doesn't return the origin — it returns a nearby CDN edge, via anycast routing or DNS-based geographic answers. My TCP and TLS handshakes then complete against that close edge, so they're cheap, and cached assets are served right there. On a miss, the edge pulls from the origin once and keeps the copy. Inside each data center it's the same story as before: a reverse proxy fronting a pool behind a load balancer."

Faster and tougher: "Faster because responses come from an edge kilometers away instead of an origin continents away — every round trip shrinks. Harder to kill because no single machine or data center matters much: anycast reroutes traffic to the next-nearest site during failures, caches absorb repeat demand without touching the origin, and DDoS floods get dispersed across hundreds of sites instead of concentrating on one target."
